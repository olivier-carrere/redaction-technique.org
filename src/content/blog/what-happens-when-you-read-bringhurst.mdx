## The book that should have stayed on the shelf

Robert Bringhurst's *The Elements of Typographic Style* is the canonical typographer's reference. It is roughly as mild-mannered as a LaTeX error log. Reading it while in the middle of a book-production sprint is not a decision a rational person makes after already running twenty commits on type parameters.

I made it.

What follows is the commit journal for the session that resulted.

## Poem spacing

Every `\poemeinline` had been generating an extra blank line after it — a `\vspace{\baselineskip}` left over from an earlier structural pass. Replaced with a bare `\par`.

Bringhurst would have noticed. The blank line is gone.

This is the kind of thing that looks fine when you produce it and obvious in retrospect. A blank line after every poem had been sitting in the layout through twenty typographic commits. The fix took five minutes. The noticing took reading Bringhurst on the relationship between whitespace and rhythm in continuous text.

## Lettrines

Drop caps gained `lhang=1`. They now hang into the margin instead of pushing body text inward.

Bringhurst explains at some length that margins are not wasted space. They are part of the page's architecture — a visual buffer that makes the text area feel contained rather than exposed. Drop caps that push body text inward collapse part of that buffer. Drop caps that hang into the margin leave the text area intact.

This is a one-parameter change. The effect across every chapter opener in all three volumes is immediate on the next build.

## Dashes

Two hyphens (`--`) serving as en dashes in two source files were corrected to actual en dashes (`–`).

Bringhurst spends more words on the distinction between hyphens, en dashes, and em dashes than seems warranted. He is right to. The wrong dash is a small error that signals to a careful reader that someone was approximating. The correct dash signals that someone was paying attention.

## The typo

One instance of "poumonsn" corrected to "poumons" in a 2013 source file. Not Bringhurst's department. But no less satisfying.

## New commands: `\separateur` and `\poemopen`

The rotating cast of ding ornaments — `✦`, `❖`, and several others that had accumulated across the sprint — was replaced by a consistent `\separateur` command. Consistency in ornaments is a detail that most readers will never consciously notice and will always subconsciously register.

The opening poem of each commentary now flows from a `\poemopen` command that calls a Lua script reading the source poem file directly. The first implementation had catcode problems with accented characters — French being reliably inconvenient in this regard. The fix involved writing to a temporary file and using `\input`. The kind of solution that feels obvious after enough staring and opaque in retrospect.

## Section circles

The enso circles that open each section had been sized for a larger page format. They needed to move to the physical page centre rather than the text-area centre, and several other adjustments followed:

- Diameter reduced 20%
- Node repositioned via `remember picture / overlay`
- Poem lines unified to the same left margin
- Runover verse lines set ragged-right with a hanging indent
- Body font stepped progressively from `\LARGE` down through `\large`, `\footnotesize`, and finally `\scriptsize`

Bringhurst would probably classify the enso as decorative. He would not be wrong.

## Frontispiece radial

The overview diagram had lost its numerical poem labels and curvilinear title in a previous session. Both were restored. Curvilinear title radius: first 3.5 cm, then revised to 3.2 cm. Smaller, better. 3.5 cm looked confident; 3.2 cm looks composed.

## Index indentation

Continuation lines were more deeply indented than sub-entries, inverting the visual hierarchy across the entire index. Fixed by redefining `\@idxitem`, `\subitem`, and `\subsubitem` via `\makeatletter`. 

Bringhurst has views on index typography. He always does. On this particular point — that sub-entries should be more indented than continuation lines, which continue a sub-entry, not a new one — he is clearly right and the default LaTeX behaviour was clearly wrong.

## Workflow schemas and metadata card

Three TikZ diagrams were overflowing their margins. `\resizebox` to each. Icon–duration pairs in the metadata card were wrapped in `\mbox{}` to prevent justification from separating an icon from its number across a line.

## The unreasonable position

None of this changed the book structurally. The reader will not notice.

That is, depending on your philosophy, either the whole point of typography or its most persistent frustration. Bringhurst holds that the reader not noticing is precisely the goal — that typography succeeds by being invisible, by presenting content without friction, without drawing attention to itself. This is a defensible position. It is also a position that leads to twenty commits on type parameters and then, later, a second pass triggered by reading a book most people give up on after the preface.

**Practical recommendation:** if you are producing a typeset document, *Thinking with Type* by Ellen Lupton is a useful baseline and a readable book. It will make your work noticeably better with a few hours of reading.

*The Elements of Typographic Style* will also make your work better. Do not read it unless your commit log has room to spare and you have made peace with caring intensely about things no one has asked you to care about.
