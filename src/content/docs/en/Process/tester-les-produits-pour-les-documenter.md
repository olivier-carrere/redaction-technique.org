---
title: "Testing products to document them."
description: "A Chinese tale tells how blind people came face to face with an elephant."
---


The **technical editor** can't provide useful documentation for the company's customers if he's content to put together information gleaned from the company's various players. Playing the role of Candide, he or she is the first representative of users, and has a duty to test products in conditions close to their own.

A Chinese tale recounts how a group of blind men were confronted by an elephant. None of them, and for good reason, had a global perception of the animal, so each had a different image of it: the one who held a leg took it for a tree, the one who embraced the trunk mistook it for a snake, the one who grabbed a tusk identified it with a spear, and the one who clutched one of its ears thought it was a fan.

The **technical writer** who asks the various players in the company what the product he's to create documentation for is for, and how it works, finds himself like the one who asks the blind what an elephant looks like: for R&D, it's elegantly written code; for marketing, an offer to position against the competition in its market; for technical support, an executable whose bugs need to be corrected, and so on.

To have a realistic vision of the object they're supposed to be describing, **technical writers** need to apprehend it for themselves and form their own opinions, which they can then compare with those of other people in the company. The **technical writer** is a pragmatist, interested in practice, not theory. If he consults only with developers, for example, he will have little chance of creating satisfactory documentation for the user:

- on the one hand, developers often have an idealistic vision of how their product should work, which differs from how it actually behaves in real-life conditions,
- on the other hand, information is necessarily lost between :
  - what the developer knows,
  - what the developer expresses,
  - what the **technical editor** understands,
  - what the **technical editor** writes,
  - what the user understands.

If the **technical writer** actually tests the product's behavior in conditions as close as possible to those encountered by the user, the first three causes of information loss are practically non-existent. To reduce the last two, all that's left is to filter, organize and express the information he has gathered in a way that is adapted to the medium through which it will be conveyed, and to the technical knowledge of the recipient.

In practice, such a request may at first seem incongruous internally, and come up against the cumbersome task of setting up a test platform. It's usually only after the first customer feedback or product tests in the press that the various parties involved fully understand the benefits of this approach. It's often only then that technical writing earns its spurs. And technical documentation is seen not just as a necessary evil, but as a genuine added value.
